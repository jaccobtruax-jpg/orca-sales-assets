OrcaRouter AE Hiring Interview Kit

How to interview candidates for an OrcaRouter account executive role. Built from the playbook, the brand voice, and the competitive KB. The through-line: Orca's brand is honesty ("Proof, not vibes"). The interview should test honesty, not smoothness. A candidate who overclaims in the interview will overclaim in front of a CTO, and this product punishes that faster than most.

The role in one paragraph (say this to the candidate)

We sell an AI gateway. One API endpoint, 200+ models, automatic failover, learned routing that cuts inference cost, and built-in security screening. Zero markup on model costs; revenue comes from a subscription, so our incentives point the same way as the buyer's. Buyers are engineering leaders, CTOs, and heads of AI at companies spending $10K+ a month on AI, mostly in the enterprise 20-account playbook. Seller identity is FlashLabs, with OrcaRouter as one product of four.

Question bank

Stage 1: Screen (C1 basic, C5)

  • "Walk me through the last six months of your day job. What did you sell, to whom, and what did you learn?" — Strong: concrete numbers, named buyers, specific lessons. Red flag: vibes, "I'm a relationship builder."
  • "When a company spends $50K a month on AI APIs, where does the money go?" — Strong: model usage, tokens, provider pricing, latency/quality tradeoffs, prompt volume. Red flag: "I don't really follow the AI space."
  • "What is a model router?" — Strong: picks the right model/provider per request for quality, cost, latency; failover; something about routing intelligence. Red flag: "like a VPN for AI." (Half credit for the VPN answer if they immediately correct themselves.)
  • "Why do you want this job specifically?" — Strong: something about the product, the market timing, or the zero-markup model. Red flag: "I love AI, and sales is sales."

Stage 2: Product and market (C1 deep, C2, C3)

  • "Explain the zero-markup model to me like I'm a CFO. How does OrcaRouter make money?" — Strong: subscription funds the platform, $0 per token, incentives aligned, and they know the honest caveat: BYOK traffic may carry a platform fee and Enterprise is quoted individually. Red flag: "we make money on volume" or "we don't."
  • "Name the three pillars and give me a buyer for each." — Strong: Reliability (customer-facing AI teams), Security (fintech/healthcare/enterprise), Cost (high-volume API consumers). Red flag: can only remember the cost pitch.
  • "A prospect says they already use OpenRouter. What do you do?" — Strong: doesn't trash them; knows the market just priced OpenRouter at $7.5B; pivots to incentive and learning: fees on spend vs subscription, market-share routing vs learned routing, and governance. Red flag: "OpenRouter is a middleman, you should ditch them."
  • "We were just acquired-ish adjacent: Stripe bought OpenRouter. How does that change your pitch?" — Strong: it validates the category, it makes "independent neutral router" a claim to prove, and they know our answer is proof, not posture. Red flag: no idea the acquisition happened.
  • "Where do we rank on the RouterArena leaderboard, and why does that matter?" — Strong: knows it is #12 of 31 (as of Aug 2026), accuracy 75.54 at $1.00/1K, that the number is a snapshot not a birthright, and that we pitch the accuracy-cost frontier among commercial routers. Red flag: quotes "#2" or "#8" from a stale source. (This is a trap question on purpose. The honest answer includes "it changes; I would check the live board.")
  • "What are our honest weak spots?" — Strong: benchmarks are self-reported, Weave beats our raw accuracy at slightly lower cost, provider count is narrower than advertised breadth, LiteLLM is genuinely free. Red flag: "we don't have any" or "I don't see any."
  • "What is a floor commit, and when would you use it?" — Strong: prospect commits a percentage of monthly AI spend, paid upfront for token volume; a floor on routed spend, not an extra fee; used when the savings story is proven. Red flag: "a retainer we use to discount."
  • "Your pricing page says $49 a month for Team. A $10K/mo prospect asks for a discount. What do you say?" — Strong: "Our fee is $0.00 on every plan. The question is which plan features you need." They do not discount $0. Red flag: "I'd find room on the Team tier."

Stage 3: Case presentation (C2 research, C3, C4)

The take-home mirrors what the current reps are doing:

Pick one competitor (OpenRouter or LiteLLM). Research it from primary
sources: their pricing page, docs, and the live RouterArena board. Produce a
one-page case study: who they are, how they make money, where they beat us,
where we beat them, and three landmine questions you would ask a prospect
who uses them. Cite everything. Present it in 15 minutes.

Stage 4: Hiring manager (C4, C5)

  • "Tell me about a deal you lost and what you actually did after."
  • "Walk me through your pipeline from one quarter. How did you qualify, and what did you kill?" — Strong: names deals they walked away from.
  • "A CTO says: we'll build this in-house. You have one response." — Strong: "Most teams spend 3-6 months building multi-model routing, streaming failover, and stream-safe guardrails. Then they own re-benchmarking a moving catalogue forever. That is a headcount line, not infrastructure."
  • "How do you handle a week where every demo goes to voicemail?"
  • "What does honest selling mean to you? Give me a time it cost you something."

Role-play scenarios (Stage 2, last 15 minutes)

Give the candidate a buyer card and play the prospect. Do not help them.

  • The OpenRouter shop. CTO at a startup spending $30K/mo through OpenRouter. Says: "OpenRouter is cheap and it just works. Why would I move?" Strong: acknowledges the product, asks about spend and reliability, lands the fee math and the learning difference, offers a replay pilot on their logs. Weak: trashes OpenRouter, no discovery.
  • The in-house builder. VP Eng who built a simple router. Says: "We handle fallback ourselves." Strong: digs for partial-failure handling ("what about garbage instead of errors?"), security screening, and the cost of maintaining it. Weak: accepts the premise.
  • The skeptical CFO. Says: "Zero markup is a gimmick. Nobody works for free." Strong: explains subscription economics calmly, shows the pricing table, does not get defensive. Weak: waffles on the model, offers a discount.

Hiring traps

  • Hiring the smoothest talker. This product punishes overclaiming in front of engineers. Listen for "let me check" as a reflex, not a weakness.
  • Hiring for AI hype fluency without product grounding. "I use ChatGPT daily" is not C1.
  • Ignoring the stale-number test. A candidate quoting an old leaderboard rank is either lazy or dishonest, and both are disqualifying at C3.
  • Hiring someone who cannot handle "we'll build in-house." This is the most common objection in the playbook and the easiest to counter. If they freeze, they will freeze on the call.
  • Not checking references for pipeline discipline. Ask for a deal they killed and what the numbers were.

Reference-check questions

  • "What was their best quarter and what drove it?"
  • "Tell me about a deal they lost and how they handled it."
  • "Did they overpromise to customers or to you?"
  • "If they had one weakness, what would it be?"
  • "Would you hire them again, and why or why not?"

Post-offer onboarding seed (share on day 1)

Give the new AE these five things before anything else: the 30-second pitch, the three pillars, the four pitches with their buyers, the objection table, and the live-research rule. The 30-second pitch to memorize:

"OrcaRouter is an AI gateway. One API endpoint that connects to 200+ models,
handles failover when models go down, saves 30-50% on AI costs, and screens
every prompt for security. Zero markup on model costs. One line of code.
Companies spending $10K+/month on AI save thousands immediately."

Then: the two hard rules. You do not discount $0. And you never quote a competitor's number or a leaderboard rank from memory; you check it live, and you label it vendor-reported when it is.
