GTM Playbook for Repsv4

Competitor-aware · technical buyers · social-first · benchmarked messaging — October 2026

The thesis: this category is won with specifics, not spray. A technical buyer decides whether to read your message in under two seconds, and the pattern-match is brutal — generic openers read as spam, specifics read as respect. Every message that books a meeting contains a number, a named model, or a named buyer. Every message that could go to anyone goes to no one.
The seven rules
1 · The money number leads.

Lead with a number, not an adjective. "Cutting-edge routing" means nothing to an engineer; "0% markup on tokens" means something. Buyers in this category respect a number over a claim, so put ours up front every time: 0% markup, 30–40%+ net savings, 200+ models.

2 · The wedge is OpenRouter's own FAQ.

Our best sales tool is the competitor's own documentation. OpenRouter says there's "no markup on inference pricing" — then charges 5.5% on every credit top-up, 5% on crypto, 5% on BYOK usage above $25K a month, and lets credits expire after a year. That's a markup with a different name, and it's all written down on their site. Quote their words, not ours — it's verifiable, and it's damning.

3 · The Stripe acquisition is the trigger.

Stripe bought OpenRouter for roughly $7.5B in August 2026. That ends the "neutral referee" story — the referee is now owned by the house accountant. Any enterprise prospect routing through OpenRouter has a governance question they can't answer, and that's your reason to reach out now, not later.

4 · Proof, not vibes.

It's our brand voice and it's also how technical buyers evaluate vendors: they verify everything, they trust peers over marketing, and they can smell a claim without evidence from across the room. Fear converts short-term; proof converts long-term. Benchmarks, docs, a live test on their stack — that's what survives contact with engineers.

5 · Never overclaim.

One wrong number ends the evaluation. A technical buyer will check the fee math, the benchmark, the latency claim — and a false one reads as either laziness or dishonesty, both fatal. Saying "I'd verify that and get back to you" is a strength, not a weakness. It's the difference between losing a deal and losing credibility forever.

6 · Persistence is the advantage.

Seventy percent of senders quit after the first touch, and replies concentrate on touches two and three — which means most senders never see the replies their own message earned. Run the full three-touch sequence on every prospect, every time. Persistence is a measurable edge, and most of the competition simply doesn't have it.

7 · Track reply rate per template.

The category average reply rate for cold outreach is about 3.4%; the top decile runs 10.7%. That spread is the whole game. Track every template you send: kill anything under 2%, scale anything over 8%, and let the data decide what you send next week.

How OpenRouter's machine works — so you can counter it

OpenRouter built the category, and their machine is worth understanding because every piece of it is counterable. They created a one-line identity — "The Unified Interface for Every Model" — simple enough that developers repeat it for them. They grew developers first: free tier, strong docs, and self-reported numbers of roughly 10 million developers and 10 trillion tokens a day before any commercial gravity. Their marketplace is a content engine: every model page and featured app is a landing page that ranks itself in search, so the catalog does the marketing. And their fee structure looks free while being a renamed markup — which is exactly the crack we sell into.

The counter in one line: OpenRouter routes. OrcaRouter governs. They route by crowd behavior; we route by learned, per-query selection. They charge fees with a different name; we charge zero markup. They just got bought by a payments company; we're independent. Every one of those is a sentence a prospect can verify.

The six rules for technical buyers
  1. Competence before product. Speak their language — models, tokens, latency, SLAs, failure modes. One sharp technical observation beats three value props.
  2. Proof over claims. The demo isn't the pitch; the docs, the GitHub, and the hands-on test are. If it can't be tested, it doesn't exist to them.
  3. Respect the evaluation. They will test it. Make the self-serve path excellent and let the product sell; sales supports, never pushes.
  4. Never overclaim. Technical buyers verify everything and pattern-match hype instantly. One false number ends the evaluation permanently.
  5. Answer build-vs-buy head-on. "Most teams spend 3–6 months building multi-model routing, streaming failover, and guardrails. Then they own re-benchmarking a moving catalogue forever. That's a headcount line, not infrastructure."
  6. Arm the champion. The technical buyer who loves the product still has to sell it internally. Give them the comparison table, the fee math, the security posture, the TCO one-pager.

The three-touch social sequence (per prospect — the floor, not the ceiling)
  1. Comment. A genuine thought on their post, referencing a specific point. No product.
  2. Engage again within about a week — a comment or a sharp public reply on a relevant thread.
  3. Warm DM. Reference both touches. One question. Under 90 words. Small ask.

Seventy percent of senders quit after touch one, and replies concentrate on touches two and three. The sequence is the advantage.
