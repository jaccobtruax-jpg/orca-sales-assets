1. What the job actually is

Selling is not talking people into things. If that were the job, the best sellers would be the best talkers, and they are not. The job is three things:

  1. Find people who have a problem you can actually fix. Most of your work is here.
  2. Understand that problem better than they do. This is the part beginners skip because they are eager to talk about the product.
  3. Help them decide. Not pressure. Help. Give them the information and the confidence to make a call they were already inclined to make.

The four things every sale needs

WhatWhat it meansWhat it looks like when it is missing
A real problemSomething is costing them money, sleep, or credibility right now.They are polite and interested and never move.
Someone who can say yesYou know who signs, and you have met them or know how to.Great conversations that cannot become signatures.
A next stepA specific action, with a date, agreed at the end of every call.The deal sits still for two months and quietly dies.
A reason for nowA real, dated event that makes this urgent."Let's revisit next quarter" forever.
The uncomfortable part Most of this job is being told no, or getting no answer at all. That is not failure, it is the shape of the work. The people who last in sales are the ones who stopped taking it personally early. You are not being rejected, your message is being ignored, and those are different things. The fix for a quiet pipeline is always more activity, not a better mood.

What you control, and what you do not

Yours to control

  • How many people you contact today
  • Whether you researched them first
  • How well you listened
  • Whether every call ended with a next step
  • Whether every number you said was true

Not yours, stop carrying it

  • Their budget cycle
  • Their reorganisation
  • Their boss saying no
  • Whether they reply today
  • What a competitor promised them

Beginners burn out trying to control the right-hand column. Spend your energy on the left one, and the right one gets easier, because you stop wasting weeks on people who were never going to buy this quarter.

2. The scoreboard: pipeline math

Sales has one genuinely fair quality: the arithmetic is honest. Every deal you close came from a chain of smaller numbers, and each number has a rate. When a month goes badly you do not need to guess why. You count backwards and find which rate broke.

The chain Conversations → Meetings → Opportunities → Closed
In plain words You talk to a number of people. Some agree to a real meeting. Some of those turn into a live opportunity with a date and a budget. Some of those sign. Each arrow has a ratio, and the ratios are learnable.
Try it with your own target Change any number below and watch what it does to your required daily activity. Most new reps are surprised by how much of the job is the first box.

Notice what the tool does not include: a field for talent, or luck, or a better script. At this stage of your career the only lever you fully own is the first box, which is why your first weeks are measured on activity rather than revenue.

The trap in the arithmetic It is tempting to fix a weak month by promising a faster close rate. You cannot. The only honest fixes are more activity at the front, or better qualification so you stop spending meetings on people who were never going to buy. Improving the quality of your conversations is the real skill, and it is what the rest of this page is about.

4. Prospecting: finding people who have the problem

You cannot sell to everyone, and trying to is the most common beginner mistake. Your first job is deciding who is worth your time, and the second is saying something worth their attention.

Use the buyer's words, not ours Internal analysis of how our buyers actually describe their own problem: workflow automation (42%) and agents (26%) dominate. Almost nobody describes it as inference (5.5%) or cost (3%). If you open with routing, inference, or per-token cost, you are using our language and their eyes will glaze. Lead with the workflow they are trying to build, land on the model layer second, and let price arrive third, if at all.

Who fits, and who to skip

Worth your time

  • Teams shipping an AI feature or agent into production now
  • Anyone with a real monthly model bill they cannot break down
  • Companies who have been burned by a provider outage
  • Anyone whose security team has to approve the AI work
  • Teams already using more than one provider

Skip, or park

  • Companies with no AI work at all yet
  • Someone with no authority over the AI roadmap
  • Other routers and gateways. They are competitors, not buyers.
  • Infrastructure suppliers who would be partners instead
  • Anyone whose only interest is a discount conversation

Why now: the only legitimate reason to reach out

An outreach message without a reason for today is spam, no matter how well written. The reason must be real, checkable, and dated. If you make one up, the buyer can tell, and you lose the relationship before it starts.

Kind of triggerWhat to look for
Money movedFunding, acquisition, a new AI budget. They just got a reason to build.
Pain feltAn outage, a rate-limit incident, a cost spike, or a public post about one of those.
CalendarA launch, a migration, a compliance deadline that forces a decision.
Warm signalThey visited, downloaded, attended, or replied to something of ours.
One trigger per touch, and name it Reference the specific thing you saw, in their words. "You mentioned the rate-limit incident in your changelog last week" beats "I saw your company is growing". The first proves you looked. The second proves you sent the same message to four hundred people.

The first message

Four rules. Short, one reason, one ask, nothing you cannot back up.

Reads like this Subject: your retry logic after the March rate limits

You wrote in your changelog that you added retries after the March rate-limit failures. Most teams end up maintaining that retry path forever.

We sit in front of the model providers and move a failed request to another one, so the retry logic stops being your problem. Worth fifteen minutes to see whether it fits?

Jaccob

What kills a first message Three paragraphs about our company. A feature list. "Just following up" with no new information. Fake urgency. A claim you cannot source. And any sentence that would read identically if you pasted in a different company name.

5. Prospecting on X

Most of our larger leads come from X. That is not chance. Our buyer has a specific problem they talk about in public, in their own words, long before they will take a call from a stranger. A rate limit. An outage. A model bill nobody can explain. By the time a company is filling in an evaluation spreadsheet, the useful conversation on X already happened weeks earlier.

That makes X unlike every other channel here in one important way: the reason to reach out is already written down, publicly, by the person you want to talk to. Your job is to find it, and then to be worth replying to.

Where this guidance comes from, and what it does not cover The search operators below come from X's own developer documentation on building queries, and the direct-message rules from X's help centre. Both retrieved 24 September 2026. Two help centre pages would not load, so treat the operator list as authoritative and the search box inside the app as close to it but not identical. You will notice there is nothing here about how X ranks posts. That is deliberate: we have not verified it, and a rep should not be repeating claims about an algorithm to a buyer who builds things for a living.

What X gives you that email cannot

What you getWhy it matters
A dated reason for nowThey posted the problem, publicly, on a specific day. That is a real trigger you can reference, not a manufactured one.
The person, not a roleNo gatekeeper and no contact form. You are talking to the engineer or the founder who feels the problem.
Evidence of what they runTheir posts tell you which providers, which stack, whether they build in house, and whether they are technical enough to care about your answer.
Their own vocabularyYou can copy how they describe the problem instead of guessing. This is worth more than any script.

What X does not give you

  • Volume. You cannot send two hundred a day. Ten thoughtful replies will outperform a hundred generic ones, and there is no tool that changes that.
  • Privacy. Every reply is public. A bad reply is a public bad reply, and buyers in a niche all know each other.
  • Permission to skip discovery. A warm post is not a qualified deal. It is an opening, and the discovery still has to happen.
The mindset shift On email you are interrupting. On X you are joining a conversation that was already happening. That difference changes everything about what works: you contribute first and ask second, and the ask gets smaller, not bigger.

You cannot cold-message most people on X, and the next tab explains exactly why in X's own words. That constraint is a gift, because it forces the behaviour that actually works: you become a familiar name in their replies first, and the message you eventually send is a warm one.

Reads like this "Worth knowing: that timeout is usually the provider's queue, not your code. Why you cannot just send a message

This is documented, not folklore. From X's help centre, you can start a conversation with anyone who follows you. For someone who does not follow you, you can only reach them if one of these is true:

  • They have opted in to receive message requests from everyone
  • They are verified and have opted in to messages from verified accounts, and you are verified
  • They are a business account that has enabled messages from anyone
  • You are both members of the same Premium Business or Organization

Otherwise your message arrives as a request, held separately from their inbox, and they decide whether to accept it.

The part that decides everything X applies a quality filter to message requests that hides the ones it judges lower quality, and you are not notified when that happens. A cold message from an unknown account, carrying a link and a pitch, is exactly the shape that filter is built to catch. So the honest conclusion is not that cold messages are rude. It is that a cold message from a stranger can be silently discarded and you will never know it happened, which is why the reply-first sequence in the previous tab is not a politeness ritual. It is the only version of this that reliably arrives.

The message, once you have earned it

Reads like this "I replied on your rate-limit thread last week about pinning to a session for the cache. You mentioned the retries now live in your own code, so I did not want to just pitch you. If it is useful I can show you what the same failover looks like when it is not your team maintaining it. Fifteen minutes, and I will say so up front if it does not fit."

Four things it does: it reminds them where they met you, it shows you read their post properly, it makes a claim we can defend, and it gives them permission to say no. That last one is not weakness. It is the reason people reply.

If it lands in Requests

  • The request is not a rejection. Do not follow up with a second message asking if they saw it.
  • Keep replying publicly in the meantime. When they see your name in a thread they care about, the request gets read.
  • If they accept, do not open with a meeting ask. Have the conversation they were already having in public.
A note on read receipts before you send anything X shows read receipts to the other side by default. They will know when you have read their message. That is another reason not to send a message you would not want read carefully.

6. Discovery: the highest-value skill you will learn

If you only get good at one thing this year, make it this. Discovery is the conversation where you ask and they talk. You are trying to find out what is actually broken, what it costs them, and who cares. You are not presenting anything.

The single most useful number in selling Aim for a talk ratio of roughly 45 percent you, 55 percent them. Practically every beginner runs at 80 percent and does not notice. If you are talking, you are not learning, and you cannot sell to something you do not understand.

Four moves that make people keep talking

MoveWhat you doHow it sounds
LoopRepeat their words back, using their vocabulary."So the retries are fine in staging and only fail in production."
MirrorReflect the emotion, not the content."That sounds like it was a rough week."
LabelName the pain you think you are hearing."It sounds like the real cost is engineering time, not the bill."
SilenceSay nothing after they answer. Count to three.Most people fill a pause with the most useful sentence of the call.

Write while they talk. It shows you are listening, and it means your notes are theirs rather than yours.

The five questions that carry a first call

  1. "What are you building right now?" Start on their work, never on our product.
  2. "What does the AI part look like today?" Which providers, who owns it, how much of it is manual.
  3. "What is the most annoying part of that?" The word annoying gets you truth. "Challenges" gets you a rehearsed answer.
  4. "What happens when it breaks?" This is where the real cost lives, in engineering time and customer trust.
  5. "If that were solved, what would it let you do that you cannot do today?" Their own words for the value, which you will reuse later.
The beginner's mistake, in one line Pitching during discovery. The moment you start describing features, you have stopped learning and they have stopped telling you the truth. There is a time to present. It is not now, and it is always later than you want it to be.

Is this real? Qualify before you invest

Three questions to ask yourself after any first call. If you cannot answer all three, the deal is not ready for a demo.

Problem

Can I describe their problem in one sentence, in their words, with a number attached?

Power

Do I know who signs, and have I at least identified how to reach them?

Plan

Is there a dated next step, and a plausible reason this happens this quarter?

Qualify out fast, and treat it as a win Handing a deal back costs you a week. Carrying a deal that cannot close costs you a quarter and distorts your forecast. Telling your manager "this one is not real, here is why" early is a sign of a good rep, not a failing one.

7. Presenting and demoing

A sale moves through four stages in one direction. Beginners lose deals by running them out of order, usually by presenting before they have discovered anything.

costs you nothing but time

Open: earn the next five minutes

Set an agenda, state why you are there, and be brief. Do not open with your company history. You are trying to earn the right to continue, nothing more.

Say it like this "I have fifteen minutes. I want to understand how you handle model providers today, and if it looks relevant I will show you one thing at the end. If it does not, I will say so and we can stop. Fair?"

That last sentence buys you enormous credibility, because almost nobody offers it.

the part that decides the deal

Discover: they talk, you listen

Everything from the previous section happens here. Use the five questions, use the four moves, keep the talk ratio near 45 percent, and write things down.

Come out with one sentence You are finished discovering when you can say their problem back to them in one sentence that contains their words and a number. If you cannot, keep asking.
the only part that costs model spend

Present: agree the target first, then show one thing

Before you show anything, get them to agree on what would make it worth their time. This is the step beginners skip, and it is why their demos wander.

Say it like this "If I could show you a way to stop maintaining your own retry logic, is that worth fifteen minutes?"

Then show that, and only that. One pillar. A tour of every feature teaches nothing and is remembered as "impressive but I am not sure what it does".

The money moment

For us, it is one line. Swapping the base address in their existing code is the moment the idea becomes real. Show that, then show the response headers that name which model actually served the request, and the per-request cost. Those two things are the difference between a claim and evidence.

costs you nothing, wins the most

Close: agree the next step, every single time

A close is not always a signature. Early in a deal, closing means agreeing the next specific action with a date. If a call ends without one, the deal has stalled and you did not notice.

Say it like this "Can we get thirty minutes with the engineer who owns this on Tuesday, and I will bring the retry example with your numbers in it?"
Stage 1 of 4

The pilot is the real close

With a technical buyer, the signature comes last. What they actually buy first is a test. Offer one, on their terms, and you stop asking them to take your word for anything.

StageWhat happens
Week 1Run it offline on their own logs. No production traffic, no commitment, nothing at risk.
Weeks 2 to 3Shadow mode. Real traffic is evaluated but nothing is blocked or changed.
Week 4A small slice of live traffic. The numbers are now theirs, not ours.
The line that closes technical buyers "You run it on your logs, with your evaluation. If it misses the threshold you set, you walk and you keep the measurement." No promise required, because the buyer is now doing the measuring.

Objections

An objection is not a rejection. It is either a real gap you need to know about, or a question in disguise. Answer the version they asked, then find out what is underneath.

  1. Ask before answering. "What is driving that concern?" gets you the real objection. The first one is usually a decoy.
  2. Never argue. You can be right and lose the deal. Agree with the concern, then reframe it.
  3. Pivot to the pilot. Almost every objection is cheaper to answer with a test than with a sentence.

8. Follow-up, and why hygiene is the whole game

Deals rarely die from a clear no. They die quietly, from nobody following up, and from notes that were never accurate enough for the next person to act on.

Here is a real example from our own pipeline review, with the accounts removed because this page is not the place for them. Four live deals were found sharing an identical next-step date of 14 July, and identical wording: check for a reply, and call if there is none. A single batch of touches had been logged on 10 July and never worked. By the time anyone looked, those four deals had been silent for 71 days.

What that example is teaching you Nobody decided to abandon those deals. A task was written down, assigned to a date, and skipped, and the pipeline still showed them as live. That is what bad hygiene looks like from the inside: not chaos, just a tidy list of things that did not happen. Your notes are the only reason anyone can tell the difference.

Rules that prevent it

  • Every call ends with a next step and a date. If they will not commit to one, that is your answer about the deal.
  • Write the note the same day. Tomorrow you will remember the gist and lose the details that mattered.
  • Record the reason, not just the action. "Sent proposal" teaches nothing. "Sent proposal because they need board approval on the 12th" tells the next person what to do.
  • Follow up on time, or change the date. A date you miss silently is worse than one you moved honestly.
  • Use more than one channel, over time. Email, then a call, then a message. Same reason each time, in different words.
  • Multi-thread early. One contact means one person who can leave, get promoted, or go quiet.
The honest measure of your work At the end of any week, the question is not "how many calls did I make". It is "does every live deal in my name have an accurate date and a real next step, and did I do the things I said I would do on the days I said I would". That is the whole job at this stage.

9. What not to say, and what to say instead

Each of these is a real line that has been said or nearly said. Being caught on one costs you the deal and the account, because the buyer can check most of them in under a minute.

"We work with 40+ providers"

What it meansThe live catalog has 15 provider prefixes. The number on some of our own pages does not match its own lists.
Why it mattersIt is checkable in our public docs, and a rep who inflates the provider count has told the buyer that every other number is suspect too.
Say insteadQuote the model count, and date it: "roughly 200 models in the catalog today, across 15 providers."

"You pay nothing on top, ever"

What it meansThe $0.00 routing fee applies to Hacker and Team. BYOK may carry a platform fee, and Enterprise is quoted individually.
Why it mattersThe first Enterprise quote makes it false. The buyer who asks about BYOK is precisely the buyer who reads the docs.
Say instead"On Hacker and Team the routing fee is zero. If you bring your own keys there may be a platform fee, and the console shows your exact rate."

"We are SOC 2 Type II certified"

What it meansWhat exists is a certificate, not an audit report. A security reviewer will ask for the report by name.
Why it mattersAsking for the wrong document in a security review makes you look like you do not know your own product. It is a short conversation and a long recovery.
Say insteadName the artifact you actually have and offer to get it in front of their reviewer.

"We will cut your AI bill by 40%"

What it meansThat class of figure is vendor-reported, not measured on this buyer's traffic.
Why it mattersYou have just handed them a number to hold you to, from their own bill, on their own account. If it lands at 12 percent, you were wrong out loud.
Say instead"I do not know what your number is. That is what the four-week replay is for, and it runs on your logs."

"Everything can run in shadow mode first"

What it meansOnly the agent firewall has shadow mode. Guardrails have none, and fire or do not.
Why it mattersIt is a claim about how the product works, made to an engineer, about the exact feature they will configure first. They will find out in an afternoon.
Say instead"The firewall can run in shadow so you see verdicts before enforcing. For guardrails we start on audit and promote to block once you have seen the traffic."

"We guarantee 100% uptime"

What it meansNo system does. Our Enterprise SLA is 99.99%, and that is a contractual number with credits attached, not a slogan.
Why it mattersAnyone senior has heard a rep promise this and watched it break. It marks you as someone who has not operated a system in production.
Say instead"Enterprise carries a 99.99% SLA. More useful than the percentage: here is what happens to a request when a provider fails mid-call."

"Our router is smarter than your evaluation"

What it meansTeams that build AI seriously have their own test suite, and it is better than any vendor benchmark for their use case.
Why it mattersYou have just told a senior engineer their work is unnecessary. That is the fastest way to end a first call.
Say instead"Your evaluation decides, not our benchmark. We make the model layer cheaper and more reliable and we let your harness judge it."

"I will get you a discount"

What it meansWe do not discount. Committed spend is a floor on money already being spent, and it is what funds the savings.
Why it mattersOnce you offer a discount, the price was never the price, and every future conversation becomes a negotiation about the number rather than the outcome.
Say instead"The routing fee is already zero. The lever is a commitment, which is a floor on spend you are making anyway, not an extra charge."

11. Your first week

You are not expected to close anything yet. You are expected to build the habits that make closing possible later, and to ask questions without worrying about looking new. Everyone here started somewhere.

  • Read this page end to end, once, without trying to memorise it.
  • Be able to explain what we sell in thirty seconds, out loud, to someone who is not technical.
  • Be able to explain the one thing we do not do: we are not an AI vendor.
  • Learn the four pillars: reliability, cost visibility, governance, no lock-in. One sentence each.
  • Say the $0.00 fee line, and its boundary, without checking. Hacker and Team.
  • Do all three drills on this page and read every explanation, including the ones you got right.
  • Read the ten statements you must never say until you could spot them in someone else's email.
  • Write five practice first messages against real companies. Bring them to your manager for a teardown.
  • Set up two of the saved searches from the X section and reply to three things properly. Do not send a single message until you have replied in public first.
  • Book your first role play, and let the objection land badly. That is what the drill is for.
  • Ask what your activity target is, in conversations per week, and write it where you can see it.
How you will be judged early on Activity, curiosity, and honesty. Not revenue, and not polish. A rep who says "I do not know, let me find out and come back to you" is worth more than one who improvises a number. Buyers forgive not knowing. They do not forgive being misled.
If something is genuinely unclear, ask internally There are open questions about our own product that are not answered anywhere public. If a buyer asks something you cannot source, take it to your manager. Do not fill the gap with a plausible guess. An invented answer is worse than no answer, because it travels.
